Cap code “104” = 10 followed by 4 zeros, in pF = 100 000 pF = 100 nF = 0.1 µF.

Unit pairs that cancel cleanly
mA  × kΩ  = V      3 mA × 1 kΩ   = 3 V
V   ÷ kΩ  = mA     5 V  ÷ 10 kΩ  = 0.5 mA
V   × mA  = mW     3 V  × 3 mA   = 9 mW
kΩ  × µF  = ms     10 k × 100 µF = 1000 ms
µH  ÷ Ω   = µs     100 µH ÷ 0.1 Ω = 1000 µs
nC  ÷ mA  = µs     29 nC ÷ 4.5 mA = 6.4 µs

Why these work: 10⁻³ × 10³ = 1, 10³ × 10⁻⁶ = 10⁻³, and so on. Pick the pair and the exponents disappear.

Resistors

Ohm · power · LEDs · dividers

Ohm’s law & power

V = I · R
P = V · I = I² · R = V² / R
V
voltage across the resistor, volts (V)
I
current through it, amps (A)
R
resistance, ohms (Ω) = V/A
P
power turned into heat, watts (W) = V·A

DatasheetPower rating @ 70 °CToleranceMax working voltage

Worked · 100 Ω across 5 V
I = V / R  = 5 / 100      = 50 mA
P = V² / R = 25 / 100     = 0.25 W
P = I² · R = 0.05² × 100  = 0.25 W  ✓

A common through-hole resistor is rated ¼ W = 250 mW. This example sits exactly at the limit and will run hot. Keep dissipation at or below about half the rating, so use a ½ W part here.

LED series resistor

R = (Vsupply − VF) / IF
Vsupply
rail feeding the LED + resistor (V)
VF
LED forward voltage at the chosen current (V). Red ≈ 1.8–2.2 V; green/blue/white ≈ 2.8–3.3 V
IF
LED forward current (A)

DatasheetVF @ IFIF max (continuous)Luminous intensity @ IF

Worked · power LED on your L7805 board (5 V, 1 kΩ)
assume red LED, VF ≈ 2.0 V
I = (5 − 2.0) / 1000     = 3 mA
PR = I² · R = (0.003)² × 1000 = 9 mW
PLED = VF · I = 2.0 × 3 mA = 6 mW

3 mA is plenty for an indicator. Modern LEDs are clearly visible at 1–2 mA, and datasheets usually quote brightness at 20 mA.

Voltage divider

Vout = Vin · R2 / (R1 + R2)
Rsource = R1 ∥ R2 = R1·R2 / (R1 + R2)
R1
top resistor, from Vin to the output node (Ω)
R2
bottom resistor, from the output node to ground (Ω)
Rsource
the resistance the output “looks like” to whatever it drives (Thevenin resistance)

Datasheet (load side)Input bias current IIBInput resistance

Worked · 2.5 V reference for a comparator from 5 V
R1 = R2 = 10 kΩ
Vout = 5 × 10k / 20k       = 2.5 V
Rsource = 10k·10k / 20k    = 5 kΩ
load should be ≥ 10 × 5 kΩ = 50 kΩ

A load of 5 kΩ would itself sit in parallel with R2: 10k ∥ 5k = 3.33 k, so Vout = 5 × 3.33/13.33 = 1.25 V, half of what you designed.

Linear supplies

regulator heat · diode drop

Linear regulator: dissipation & junction temperature

P = (Vin − Vout) · Iout
TJ = TA + P · RthJA
Vin(min) = Vout + Vdrop
Vin
voltage at the regulator’s input pin (after any series diode) (V)
Iout
load current (A). The small quiescent current adds a little more
RthJA
thermal resistance, junction to ambient air, no heatsink (°C/W): degrees of temperature rise per watt
TA, TJ
ambient and silicon (junction) temperature (°C)
Vdrop
dropout: minimum headroom before regulation fails (V)

Datasheet (ST L7805, TO-220)RthJA 50 °C/WRthJC 5 °C/WVd 2 V typ @ 1 ATOP 0–125 °C (C grade)Id quiescent

Worked · your 9 V barrel → diode → L7805 → 5 V
Vin = 9 − 0.8 (series diode)   = 8.2 V
headroom = 8.2 − 5 = 3.2 V  ≥ 2 V ✓

@ 200 mA: P = 3.2 × 0.2      = 0.64 W
          TJ = 25 + 0.64 × 50  = 57 °C
@ 500 mA: P = 3.2 × 0.5      = 1.6 W
          TJ = 25 + 1.6 × 50   = 105 °C

efficiency (reg)   = 5 / 8.2 = 61 %
efficiency (board) = 5 / 9   = 56 %

At 500 mA it is 20 °C from the 125 °C limit with no heatsink, and thermal shutdown will cut out on a warm day. A heatsink swaps RthJA for RthJC + Rcase-sink + Rsink-air, e.g. 5 + 1 + 10 = 16 °C/W → TJ = 25 + 1.6 × 16 = 51 °C.

Diode drop & dissipation

Vafter = Vbefore − VF
P = VF · IF
VF
forward voltage. It is not a constant: it rises with current and falls as the diode warms (V)
IF
forward current (A)

Datasheet (1N4001)VF ≤ 1.1 V @ 1 AIF(AV) 1 AVRRM 50 V

Worked · reverse-polarity diode at 200 mA
VF ≈ 0.8 V at 200 mA (read off the VF–IF curve)
P = 0.8 × 0.2 = 0.16 W   (fine for a 1 A part)

A Schottky (e.g. 1N5817, VF ≈ 0.3–0.45 V) gives the regulator about 0.4 V more headroom and runs cooler.

VC(t) = Vfinal · (1 − e−t/τ)

Worked · 10 kΩ, 100 µF

Worked
100 µF @ 100 Hz:  1/(6.283×100×100e-6) = 15.9 Ω
100 nF @ 1 MHz:   1/(6.283×1e6×100e-9)  = 1.59 Ω

A ceramic cap’s real capacitance is less than its label. An X7R 10 µF / 10 V part can lose half or more of its capacitance at 5 V DC. Check the DC-bias curve and use C0G/NP0 where the exact value matters (timing, filters).

Capacitor charge, droop & energy

Q = C · V
ΔV = I · Δt / C
E = ½ · C · V²
Q
charge, coulombs (C = A·s)
C
capacitance, farads (F = C/V)
ΔV
voltage drop when current I is drawn for time Δt with no recharge (V)
E
stored energy, joules (J = W·s)
Worked · 220 µF on a 5 V rail
5 mA drawn for 10 ms, supply gone:
ΔV = 0.005 × 0.010 / 220e-6  = 0.23 V

E = ½ × 220e-6 × 5²         = 2.75 mJ

Rearranged, this is how you size hold-up caps: C = I·Δt / ΔVallowed.

Inductor current ramp

V = L · ΔI / Δt → ΔI = V · Δt / L
τ = L / R
E = ½ · L · I²
L
inductance, henries (H = V·s/A)
V
voltage across the inductor. A constant V gives a straight-line current ramp
R
series resistance: the winding’s DCR plus anything else in the loop (Ω)

Datasheet (inductor)Isat (L drops by 20–30 %)Irms (heat rating)DCR

Worked · 100 µH with 4 V across it
slope = V / L = 4 / 100e-6   = 40 000 A/s
                             = 0.04 A per µs
for 11.1 µs: ΔI = 0.04 × 11.1 = 0.44 A

τ with 0.1 Ω DCR = 100 µH / 0.1 Ω = 1 ms
E @ 0.72 A = ½ × 100e-6 × 0.72² = 26 µJ

These are the buck converter’s numbers: 4 V = 9 − 5 across the inductor during the on-time.

LC resonance

f0 = 1 / (2π · √(L · C))
f0
frequency where XL = XC (Hz)
Worked · buck output filter 100 µH + 220 µF
√(100e-6 × 220e-6) = 148.3e-6
f0 = 1 / (6.283 × 148.3e-6) = 1.07 kHz

The filter passes DC and blocks the switching frequency, so f0 should sit well below fsw. At 50 kHz, that’s about 47× higher, which is fine.

Buck converter

ideal, continuous conduction

Duty cycle, inductor ripple, output ripple

D = Vout / Vin
ΔIL = (Vin − Vout) · D / (L · fsw)
ΔVC ≈ ΔIL / (8 · fsw · Cout)
ΔVESR ≈ ΔIL · ESR
Ipeak = Iout + ΔIL / 2
D
duty cycle: fraction of each period the switch is on (0–1)
fsw
switching frequency (Hz); period T = 1/fsw, on-time = D·T
ΔIL
peak-to-peak inductor ripple current (A)
ΔVC
output ripple from charging and discharging the capacitance (V p-p)
ESR
output cap’s equivalent series resistance (Ω)

DatasheetInductor IsatCap ESRCap ripple currentDiode IF(AV), trr

Your design is hysteretic, so fsw isn’t fixed. The switch toggles whenever the output crosses the comparator’s hysteresis band, so frequency depends on the band width, the ripple and the load. These equations still give you the size of things at whatever frequency it settles to.

Worked · 9 V → 5 V, 500 mA, 100 µH, 220 µF, assume 50 kHz
D     = 5 / 9                         = 0.556
T     = 1 / 50 kHz                    = 20 µs
ton  = 0.556 × 20 µs                 = 11.1 µs
ΔIL  = (9−5) × 0.556 / (100e-6 × 50e3)
      = 2.222 / 5                     = 0.44 A p-p
Ipeak = 0.5 + 0.44/2                  = 0.72 A

ΔVC  = 0.444 / (8 × 50e3 × 220e-6)
      = 0.444 / 88                    = 5 mV
ΔVESR (ESR 0.1 Ω) = 0.444 × 0.1    = 44 mV

With a general-purpose electrolytic, ESR causes most of the output ripple (44 mV vs 5 mV here). Pick a low-ESR cap, or add a ceramic in parallel. Also check that the inductor’s Isat is above 0.72 A with margin.

Comparators & references

LM393 hysteresis · TL431

Comparator hysteresis (non-inverting)

Vrise = Vref · (1 + R1/R2)
ΔVhyst = VCC · R1/R2
Vfall = Vrise − ΔVhyst
R1
from the signal to the (+) input (Ω)
R2
feedback, from the output back to (+) (Ω)
Vref
fixed voltage on the (−) input (V)
Vrise
input level that flips the output high (V)
Vfall
input level that flips it back low (V)

Assumes output low ≈ 0 V and output high ≈ VCC. The LM393 is open-collector, so “high” comes from the pull-up resistor.

Datasheet (LM393)VOL ≤ 0.4 V @ IOL 4 mAVIO offsetResponse timeCommon-mode range

Worked · VCC 5 V, Vref 2.5 V, R1 10 k, R2 100 k
R1/R2 = 10k / 100k            = 0.1
Vrise  = 2.5 × 1.1           = 2.75 V
ΔV     = 5 × 0.1              = 0.50 V
Vfall  = 2.75 − 0.50         = 2.25 V

When the output is high, the pull-up resistor is in series with R2 (100 k + Rpu), which narrows the band slightly. Keep Rpu ≪ R2, e.g. 1–4.7 k against 100 k.

BJT drive · MOSFET losses

BJT as a saturated switch

IB ≥ IC / βforced (βforced ≈ 10)
RB = (Vdrive − VBE) / IB
IC
collector current the load needs (A)
IB
base current you supply (A)
βforced
deliberately low IC/IB ratio that drives the transistor fully into saturation. Don’t use the hFE figure here: that’s its gain in the linear region
VBE

Datasheet (2N2222A)VCE(sat) ≤ 0.3 V @ 150/15 mAVBE(sat) 0.6–1.2 VhFE 100–300 @ 150 mAIC max

Worked · your 2N2222 pulling the IRF9540 gate low
gate pull-up 1 kΩ to 9 V:
IC = 9 V / 1 kΩ               = 9 mA
IB = 9 mA / 10                = 0.9 mA
RB = (5 − 0.7) / 0.9 mA       = 4.78 k → 4.7 kΩ

If the base is driven from the LM393’s pull-up rather than a push-pull output, that pull-up resistor is in series with RB. Add it into the sum.

VGS(th) (−2 to −4 V for the IRF9540) is where the FET only just starts to conduct, at 250 µA. It isn’t the voltage for fully on. Use the VGS listed beside RDS(on). That’s why a 3.3 V GPIO can’t drive an IRFZ44N properly but can mostly drive an RFP30N06LE.

MOSFET switching loss (rough)

tsw ≈ Qgd / Ig
Ig ≈ Vplateau / Rg
Psw ≈ ½ · Vin · ID · tsw · fsw (per edge)
Qgd
gate-drain (Miller) charge: the charge that must move while drain voltage swings (C)
Vplateau
|VGS| where the gate voltage flattens during the swing, ~4–5 V for these parts
Rg
whatever resistance charges or discharges the gate (Ω)
tsw
time spent with both voltage and current on the FET (s)

Datasheet (IRF9540)Qg 61 nCQgs 14 nCQgd 29 nCGate-charge curve

Worked · turn-off through the 1 kΩ pull-up
Ig    ≈ 4.5 V / 1 kΩ            = 4.5 mA
toff  ≈ 29 nC / 4.5 mA          = 6.4 µs
period @ 50 kHz                  = 20 µs

Psw ≈ ½ × 9 × 0.5 × 6.4e-6 × 50e3
     = 0.72 W   (vs 28 mW conduction)

Turn-on is fast because the NPN sinks hard. Turn-off is slow because the 1 k pull-up does all the work, so the switching loss dwarfs the conduction loss and the FET will get warm. A 220 Ω pull-up gives about 20 mA and about 1.4 µs, but then the NPN sinks about 41 mA and needs about 4 mA of base current.
